Parameter Design Requirement

Phase Margin (PM) >
Maximum Output Capacitance 2pF
Process Library TSMC 0.18m

A 10 external reference is provided.

2. Topology Selection

Compared to the last design we discussed in GMID Methodology, this requirement is much higher. Then the typical two-stage Miller compensation does not work anymore. Then we may consider advanced techniques.

Recall that cascode can boost the output impedance, then we can apply a folded-cascode topology as the first stage. The second stage, also apply CS stage, since the load capacitance is not large. If it is designed to carry large capacitance, we may consider upgrade it to Class-AB stage.

Compensation is required for unity-gain stable. If not, the opamp will have a larger GBW but a minimum gain to maintain stable. In market such devices are called decompensated amplifier.

6. AC Response

Then add an iprobe in the testbench to run stb analysis.

AC testbench setup

The IPRB0 is a current probe used for stb analysis. Different from AC analysis, in stb analysis open-loop Bode plot is directly available even if the system is connected closed-loop. Run the simulation.

AC response of the initial values

We have reached 107 dB DC gain and 28 MHz GBW. Compared to our requirements, we have reserved too much gain margin and too little frequency margin. In the meantime, the first non-dominant pole is too low so that the phase margin is slightly insufficient. To alleviate this, we must push the dominant pole away from the origin or raise the gain while remaining the dominant pole unchanged, and the first non-dominant pole should also be pushed away.

It seems that increasing both poles at the same time is not applicable because is positively related to . Hence we may try to increase .

To increase , we prefer to increase the current instead of the channel length becuase larger gate area introducing extra parasitic capacitance, worsening the AC response. Then we widen the bias current device of the input differential pairs to . And we’ve obtained a better performance.

7. Further Adjustments on Headrooms

However the design has not been ended. After the optimization, we discover that the right branch of the first stage enters sub-threshold region.

Optimized AC response

Although it functions normally, to avoid cutoff, we should pull them back into the saturation region. The reason for entering the subthreshold region is obvious: at a fixed bias voltage, almost all of the limited current that M8 and M9 can supply is drawn by M0 and M1. Therefore, we need to find a way to increase the total current that M8 and M9 can supply. Increase their width to 40.

Solution to sub-threshold issue

Then check the stb result.

Frequency response after DC correction

The GBW and PM performance are improved but the gain falls. To compensate, we further increase the input pair current slightly. Do not forget to scale the width of the input pair while scaling the bias device. If the total current still limits the scaling, some gain can be allocated to the second stage since we have enough margin for GBW.

Final frequency response

The adjustment starts from M0 and M1. Increase the current to carry the most of the incremental gain. When the output branch in the first stage tend to carry too small current, the remaining gain is allocated to the second stage. To save current budget, we raise the channel length to 400nm instead of boosting the current. Since most increments are carried by the first stage which is independent of the poles, the GBW is also improved while the gain is raised.

After that, to improve headroom, the bias network is also adjusted.

Final setup of the opamp

Finally it reached 107dB gain and 37.3MHz GBW.

8. Slew Rate Issues

Then test the slew rate.

Testbench of slew rate

The input is applied with a pulse source with 2.3V high, 1V low, 1 period and 1ps rise/fall time. Run a trans analysis.

Waves of transition

We can calculate

It is a normal value, which performs well in our topology.

Note that there is a 300mV overshoot at the end of the falling edge. Since the second-stage NMOS continues to pull the output down. During the decline, the pull-up current from the PMOS is very small; when the output approaches the target level, the preceding stage and the compensation node cannot restore the PMOS current in time, hence the output continues to drop before being pulled back up, causing the huge overshoot.

But in small signal applications, the overshoot cannot impact the response.
